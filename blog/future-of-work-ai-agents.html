The Future of Work in the Age of AI Agents: What Changes, What Doesn't

By Omkar Vijay Ghate  ·  2026-09-15  ·  LinkedIn  ·  All Articles

Every major technology shift in history has prompted the same fear: "this machine will take our jobs." The printing press. The steam engine. The computer. The internet. Each time, the fear was partially correct — some jobs disappeared — and mostly incomplete — because the technology created far more work than it eliminated, just work of a different kind.

AI agents are different in one important way: they affect cognitive work, not just physical or mechanical work. This makes the transition more complex, more personal, and more urgent to understand.

What AI Agents Actually Replace (And What They Don't)

The first step is being precise about what is changing. AI agents excel at work that is:

  • Rule-based: The task follows predictable patterns. The same inputs reliably produce the same outputs.
  • Information-retrieval intensive: The work is primarily finding, reading, and synthesising existing information.
  • High-volume and repetitive: The same task done thousands of times with minor variations.
  • Language-based: Writing, summarising, classifying, translating, responding.

AI agents struggle with (or cannot do) work that requires:

  • Novel judgment: Situations genuinely outside training data where the right answer is not deducible from rules.
  • Physical presence: Building, repairing, assessing physical objects, performing procedures.
  • Deep human relationship: Trust built over years, cultural intimacy, emotional attunement that requires lived experience.
  • Creative vision: The kind of originality that re-defines categories rather than executes within them.
  • Ethical responsibility: Decisions where someone must be accountable — a doctor, a judge, a manager who owns outcomes.

The jobs most at risk are not the lowest-paid or the highest-paid. They are the ones in the middle: high-volume, structured cognitive tasks that were once the domain of educated professionals but are now automatable by AI.

The Indian Context: What Makes This Different Here

India's workforce has specific characteristics that shape how AI transformation will unfold here:

The IT/BPO Sector

India employs roughly 5 million people in IT services and 1.5 million in BPO/KPO. The BPO sector handles exactly the kind of structured, repetitive cognitive tasks AI agents do well. This sector will see significant displacement — but also significant new opportunity in building, managing, and overseeing the AI systems that replace the manual work.

The MSME Sector

India's 63 million MSMEs are predominantly in sectors — retail, local services, agriculture, craft — where physical presence and local relationship are irreplaceable. These businesses will be affected by AI differently: as customers of AI tools that help them compete, not as victims of AI displacement.

The Education Gradient

India's white-collar workforce has expanded enormously in the last 20 years, often into jobs that are now automatable. The challenge is significant: a generation of educated Indians entered stable careers in data processing, document management, and structured analysis. AI threatens that stability in ways that require genuine policy attention.

The Jobs That Are Growing Because of AI

Here is what is actually happening to work — not what is theoretically possible, but what I observe as someone building AI systems for Indian businesses today:

AI Agent Designers and Builders

Someone has to design, build, test, and maintain the AI agents. This requires understanding the business workflow, the technical stack, and how to make AI systems that reliably serve real customers. This is already a high-demand, well-paid role in India. Every AI agent deployed creates ongoing work for the person who built it.

AI Quality Supervisors

AI agents need human oversight. Someone monitors the conversations, catches failures, updates the knowledge base, and escalates edge cases. This is a new category of work — analytically demanding, important, and perfectly suited for people transitioning from traditional customer service or quality assurance roles.

Human-AI Collaboration Managers

In hybrid AI-human systems, someone designs and manages the handoff protocols — when does the AI escalate, how does the human agent use AI-generated suggestions, how do you measure whether the hybrid is working? This is a management function that did not exist five years ago.

Domain Experts as AI Trainers

AI systems need domain knowledge to be effective. A doctor who understands symptoms, a real estate agent who knows Mumbai's micro-markets, a tax professional who understands GST nuances — these experts are needed to translate their knowledge into training data and system prompts for AI. Their expertise becomes more valuable, not less, as it can be scaled through AI.

AI-Augmented Professionals

The fastest-growing professional category is people who are excellent at their core skill AND know how to use AI to amplify it. A financial advisor who uses AI to analyse all of their clients' portfolios simultaneously and identify the 10 who need urgent attention. A lawyer who uses AI to review 100 contracts and surfaces the three with unusual clauses. An architect who uses AI to generate 50 floor plan variations and selects the best three to refine. These professionals are not being replaced — they are becoming 10x more productive and correspondingly more valuable.

The Skills That Matter in an AI-Augmented Economy

Based on what I see in the market and in my own work, here are the skills that retain and grow in value:

Judgement and Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

When the situation is unprecedented and the AI says "I don't know," a human needs to make the call. The ability to reason through ambiguous situations, weigh competing considerations, and decide — and be accountable for that decision — is irreplaceable.

Interpersonal Trust-Building

Relationships built on genuine human connection — a doctor-patient relationship, a mentor-mentee bond, a long-term business partnership — cannot be replicated by AI. The people who build these relationships, and who understand how to navigate them, become more valuable as AI handles transactional interactions.

Creative Direction and Curation

AI can generate; it cannot direct. The person who knows what good looks like, who can distinguish between 50 AI-generated options and identify the one worth refining, who has taste — that person's role is not diminished by AI. It is amplified.

AI Literacy

Understanding what AI can and cannot do, how to prompt it effectively, how to identify its errors, and how to design workflows around its strengths and weaknesses — this meta-skill cuts across every profession and is arguably the most universally valuable skill to develop right now.

Domain Depth

Shallow generalist knowledge is increasingly handled by AI. Deep, specialised expertise in a specific domain — a particular industry, a specific legal area, a narrow technical field — becomes more valuable because it is harder for AI to replicate and more valuable when used to guide AI systems.

Practical Steps for Indian Professionals

If you are building a career in India right now, here is my honest advice:

  1. Learn AI tools in your field right now. Not to understand the theory, but to use them. Use AI for your actual work. Find out what it can and cannot do in your specific domain. This experience is more valuable than any certification.
  2. Identify the 20% of your work that AI cannot do. Every profession has tasks that require genuine human judgment, relationship, or accountability. Double down on those tasks. Become excellent at what AI cannot replace in your specific role.
  3. Build expertise in AI-adjacent skills. Prompt engineering, AI agent design, workflow automation, AI quality evaluation — these are in demand now and will be for years. None require a computer science degree.
  4. Document your domain knowledge. Your expertise, if structured well, can be used to build AI systems that serve your field. Knowing how to translate human expertise into AI training data and system prompts is a valuable skill.
  5. Stay curious and adaptive. The specific tools and roles will change faster than any single article can track. The meta-skill is the ability to learn new tools quickly, adapt your workflow, and find where your human judgment adds unique value.

The Honest Reckoning

I will not pretend the transition is painless. Some people in some roles will face genuinely difficult disruption. A 45-year-old data entry professional in a BPO in Hyderabad faces real challenges that a 22-year-old software student does not.

Policy responses — reskilling programmes, income support during transitions, education reform — are part of the answer to that challenge. But at the individual level, the most protective action is to understand the shift, build skills that complement AI rather than compete with it, and make deliberate choices about where to direct your professional energy.

The future of work is not a world without work. It is a world where the work that remains requires more of what is distinctly human — judgment, creativity, relationship, accountability, and wisdom. That is not a worse world. It is a more demanding one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which Indian jobs are most at risk from AI in the next 5 years?

The highest-risk roles are structured, high-volume cognitive tasks: data entry operators, basic document review, standard customer support queries, routine financial report generation, and repetitive content writing. Roles involving physical presence, complex judgment, relationship management, and creative direction are significantly lower risk.

Will AI create more jobs than it destroys in India?

History suggests yes — every major technology shift has created more work than it eliminated, even if the new work looks very different from the old. However, the transition period is genuinely difficult for people in disrupted roles. The net employment effect over 10 years is likely positive; the distribution of who benefits and who bears the transition cost is the real policy challenge.

What is the best skill to learn in India to be AI-proof?

No skill is completely AI-proof, but skills that are highly durable include: deep domain expertise in a specific field, interpersonal trust-building and relationship management, creative direction and taste, complex judgment under uncertainty, and AI literacy itself (knowing how to work with and direct AI systems). The combination of deep domain knowledge plus AI literacy is particularly valuable.

How should Indian businesses prepare their workforce for AI?

Start by identifying which tasks in your business are most automatable (repetitive, rule-based, information-retrieval), then invest in reskilling your people for the higher-judgment tasks that remain. Introduce AI tools gradually, with training. Design roles that leverage both AI efficiency and human judgment. Avoid the mistake of implementing AI without involving the people whose work will change — their domain knowledge is essential for making the AI actually work.

Is AI a threat to India's IT sector?

AI is transforming India's IT sector more than threatening it. The routine work of software development, testing, and documentation is increasingly AI-augmented. The result is higher productivity per developer, not fewer developers. The demand for AI-skilled developers, AI system architects, and AI product managers is growing faster than the displacement of routine coding tasks. India's IT sector needs to upskill rapidly rather than shrink.
